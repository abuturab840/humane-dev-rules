60 rules in ten sections

The rules

Short rules for building websites, apps, software and AI that treat people well.

Every rule has one of two labels:

  • Always rules apply to every project, even a small one.
  • Only if rules apply when the condition is true for your project. The intake questions help you work out which ones those are.

Some rules have an "Always" part and an "Only if" part. For example, rule 22Rule 22. Always. Meet level 1 of OWASP's security checklist for apps (ASVS). Only if you hold personal, health or payment data: meet level 2. Only if it's a mobile app: also meet OWASP's mobile checklist (MASVS). asks every project for the basic security level, and a higher level only if you hold personal data.

If a word is new to you, the glossary explains it. If you're not sure how to start, read how to use these rules first. This isn't legal advice.

A

A. Starting a project

Do these before you design anything. They take less time than you think and they save you from building the wrong thing.

  1. 1
    Rule 1. Always Answer the ten intake questions before you start. They ask things like "Does your product collect personal data?", "Could children use it?" and "Does it use AI?". Your answers show which "Only if" rules apply to you.
  2. 2
    Rule 2. Always Before you design anything, think through who your product could harm and how. This is called a Consequence Scan. Fifteen minutes is enough for a small site or feature. Give it up to an hour if the product handles personal data, could be used by children or uses AI. Write down the biggest risks and how you'll deal with each one.
  3. 3
    Rule 3. Only if you handle personal data, especially health, biometric or payment data: write down the privacy risks and how you'll reduce them before you build. This is called a privacy risk assessment (DPIA).
  4. 4
    Rule 4. Always Make a simple threat model. Write down what an attacker might want from your product, like customer data or admin access, and how you'll stop them.
  5. 5
    Rule 5. Always Pick one number that shows your product is really helping people, and track it. "Time spent in the app" is never the goal.
B

B. Humane design

How the product treats people's attention and choices.

  1. 6
    Rule 6. Only if your product sends notifications: keep them off by default, except essential ones like security alerts or booking reminders. Never use red badges to push marketing.
  2. 7
    Rule 7. Only if your product plays video or audio: don't play it automatically unless the user turns that on.
  3. 8
    Rule 8. Only if your product has a feed: give it natural stopping points, like "You're all caught up". Never use endless scrolling for anyone under 18.
  4. 9
    Rule 9. Always Make leaving as easy as joining. Cancelling, saying no and deleting an account should take no more effort than signing up, saying yes or creating one.
  5. 10
    Rule 10. Always Don't use dark patterns, the design tricks that push people into things they didn't mean to do, and don't use guilt-trip wording like "No thanks, I don't like saving money". Check your screens against the examples at deceptive.design.
  6. 11
    Rule 11. Only if your product ranks or recommends posts: reward the helpful ones, not the ones that make people angry.
C

C. Privacy and cookies

What data you collect, how long you keep it, and how you ask for consent.

  1. 12
    Rule 12. Always Only collect the data you really need, and decide when each type of data will be deleted.
  2. 13
    Rule 13. Only if users have accounts: set every privacy option to the most private choice by default.
  3. 14
    Rule 14. Only if you hold personal data: let people see their data, download it and delete it.
  4. 15
    Rule 15. Only if you have visitors from the EU or UK and use optional cookies or tracking tags: don't load anything optional until the visitor says yes.
  5. 16
    Rule 16. Only if you show a cookie banner: put "Reject all" and "Accept all" side by side on the first screen, looking exactly the same.
  6. 17
    Rule 17. Only if you have visitors from the US and you sell or share their data or run targeted ads: respect Global Privacy Control, a browser setting that says "don't sell my data", and show a "Do Not Sell or Share" link.
  7. 18
    Rule 18. Only if you have visitors from the US and use tracking: ad and analytics pixels follow rule 17Rule 17. Only if you have visitors from the US and you sell or share their data or run targeted ads: respect Global Privacy Control, a browser setting that says "don't sell my data", and show a "Do Not Sell or Share" link.. But never record visitors' screens (session replay) or chats without telling them and asking first, and never put ad pixels on sensitive pages, like health, money, children's or account pages.
  8. 19
    Rule 19. Only if you use optional cookies: put a cookie settings link in every footer, and remember each visitor's choice for six months.
  9. 20
    Rule 20. Only if you use optional cookies or tags: check every cookie your site sets every three months, and again whenever you add a plugin or tag.
D

D. Security

How you protect your users, your code and your accounts.

  1. 21
    Rule 21. Always Follow the good habits in NIST's Secure Software Development Framework (SSDF), a free US guide to building software securely.
  2. 22
    Rule 22. Always Meet level 1 of OWASP's security checklist for apps (ASVS). Only if you hold personal, health or payment data: meet level 2. Only if it's a mobile app: also meet OWASP's mobile checklist (MASVS).
  3. 23
    Rule 23. Only if users log in: check on the server, every time, that the person is allowed to do what they're asking. When in doubt, the answer is no.
  4. 24
    Rule 24. Always Protect every account that touches your code, hosting or packages with a login that can't be phished, like passkeys or security keys.
  5. 25
    Rule 25. Always Never put passwords, keys or other secrets in your code or logs. Keep them in a secrets manager and use tokens that expire quickly.
  6. 26
    Rule 26. Always Encrypt all traffic with TLS 1.2 or newer, the padlock in the browser. Only if you store passwords or sensitive data: scramble passwords with Argon2 or bcrypt, and encrypt the data you store.
  7. 27
    Rule 27. Always Make the safe setting the default: debug mode off, security headers on, and any features you don't use switched off.
  8. 28
    Rule 28. Always Use as few outside packages and plugins as you can, pick well-maintained ones, and lock their versions in a lockfile.
  9. 29
    Rule 29. Always Give every release an SBOM, a list of everything inside it in CycloneDX or SPDX format, and a digital signature.
  10. 30
    Rule 30. Always Lock down how you build and deploy. Build in a clean, separate environment, and have a person approve every release to production.
  11. 31
    Rule 31. Only if you take payments: never store card details yourself. Use a payment provider.
E

E. Testing

The checks that prove the rules above are actually working.

  1. 32
    Rule 32. Always Scan every code change automatically: the code itself (SAST), the packages it uses (SCA), and for leaked secrets and unsafe settings.
  2. 33
    Rule 33. Always Before every release, run a security scan on the live site (DAST), an accessibility check and a dark pattern review. Only if users log in: also test that each type of user can only reach what they should. Only if you use tracking: also check your cookies.
  3. 34
    Rule 34. Only if it's a big launch, or the product has been live for a year: get a penetration test, where an expert tries to break in. Also review your threat model and practise restoring from a backup.
  4. 35
    Rule 35. Always Never ship with a known critical or high security problem. If you really must make an exception, write down why and the date it runs out.
F

F. AI features

Only for products that use AI. Skip this section if yours doesn't.

  1. 36a
    Rule 36a. Only if users chat with AI or read replies it writes: show a small "AI" label, unless that's already obvious, for example on a "Generate with AI" button.
  2. 36b
    Rule 36b. Only if your AI has a human form, like a face, a human name or a voice: say clearly at the start that it's AI, never let it claim to have feelings, and remind users during long chats.
  3. 37
    Rule 37. Only if your AI makes images, audio or video: label them with C2PA Content Credentials, a tag inside the file that shows AI made it.
  4. 38
    Rule 38. Only if you use AI: treat everything that goes into the AI as untrusted, and guard against the risks in OWASP's Top 10 for AI apps (the LLM Top 10).
  5. 39
    Rule 39. Only if your AI can take actions: give it as little power as possible, and have a person approve anything that spends money, sends messages or deletes things.
  6. 40
    Rule 40. Only if your AI's answers affect money, health, jobs or legal matters: have a person check them.
  7. 41
    Rule 41. Only if you use AI: don't track people's moods, and don't use emotional pressure to keep them using it.
  8. 42
    Rule 42. Only if you use AI: try hard to make it misbehave before launch and after any big change. This is called red-teaming.
G

G. Children and accessibility

Making sure the product is safe for young people and usable by everyone, including disabled people.

  1. 43
    Rule 43. Only if children might use it: use the safest settings by default. No profiling, location off, parental consent where the law needs it, and no addictive features.
  2. 44
    Rule 44. Always Meet the WCAG 2.2 AA accessibility standard, and test it using only a keyboard and with a screen reader.
H

H. Launch

What needs to be in place on the day you go live.

  1. 45
    Rule 45. Always Publish an accessibility statement. Only if you hold personal data: publish a privacy notice. Only if you use cookies: publish a cookie policy. Only if you have accounts or payments: publish terms that explain clearly how to cancel.
  2. 46
    Rule 46. Always Publish a security.txt file and a short page explaining how people can report security problems to you.
  3. 47
    Rule 47. Only if it's an app or software people install, not just a website: say how long you'll keep releasing security updates.
  4. 48
    Rule 48. Always The person doing the release goes through the release checklist and saves the result with the release. For big launches, a second person checks it too. Working alone? Check it again the next day with fresh eyes.
I

I. After launch

Keeping the product safe once people are using it.

  1. 49
    Rule 49. Always Fix security problems quickly: within 72 hours if they're being actively attacked, 7 days if critical, 30 days if high and 90 days if medium.
  2. 50
    Rule 50. Always Have a plan for security incidents. Only if you hold personal data: also have a plan for data breaches, including reporting them within 72 hours where the law requires it.
  3. 51
    Rule 51. Only if you hold personal data: answer people's requests about their data within a month. Only if users can post content: remove reported intimate images within 48 hours.
  4. 52
    Rule 52. Always Check your wellbeing number from rule 5Rule 5. Always. Pick one number that shows your product is really helping people, and track it. "Time spent in the app" is never the goal. every month, and fix whatever is hurting people.
  5. 53
    Rule 53. Always Every three months, check who has access and remove old accounts and keys. Every year, update your risk notes and threat model, practise your incident plan and check for new laws.
  6. 54
    Rule 54. Always After any incident, write a short note covering what happened, why, and what you changed. Track each fix until it's done.

    Rule 55. Always Pass the Manipulation Matrix: only build something if you'd use it yourself and it truly makes people's lives better.
  2. 56
    Rule 56. Always In every dark pattern review, ask the regret test question: would people regret using this feature if they understood how it works? If yes, remove it.

    Rule 57. Only if your product has rewards, points or badges: make rewards earned and predictable. No random rewards designed to keep people hooked.
  4. 58
    Rule 58. Only if users can invite others: let them choose each person. Never send automatic invites from uploaded contact lists, and limit how many invites can be sent.
  5. 59
    Rule 59. Only if people use your product regularly: give them controls like quiet hours, a daily limit, snooze and optional usage summaries.
  6. 60
    Rule 60. Only if you show numbers or reviews: show only real, current figures and genuine reviews. No fake urgency like "Only 2 left!" unless it's true.
